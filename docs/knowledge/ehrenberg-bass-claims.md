**Status:** first entries. Extracted 2026-09-13 from open-access PDFs supplied by Ari.
**Purpose:** the Knowledge layer from `docs/vision.md` — claims with sources attached,

## How to read this file

Every row is a claim we can **cite**, not a claim we have **verified independently**.
The distinction matters: Ehrenberg-Bass is the most empirically serious marketing
institute there is, and it is still one institute with one research programme. Where
a number is a heuristic rather than a measurement, this file says so, because the
source says so.

**Ideas extracted, wording not reproduced.** Short quotes are attributed. Anything
longer is stated in our own words with a page reference so it can be checked.

**Nothing here is about Instagram, short-form video, or consumer content.** Every
source below is B2B. That is a real limit and §Applicability records it per claim
rather than letting it get lost.

## Claims

### C1 — The 95-5 rule

**At any one time, roughly 95% of business buyers are not in the market.**

| | |
|---|---|
| Source | *Advertising effectiveness and the 95-5 rule*, Ehrenberg-Bass B2B Report |
| Author | Prof. John Dawes |
| Date | May 2021 |
| What was measured | Derived from average interpurchase intervals, not surveyed directly. Corporations change principal bank or law firm roughly every five years, giving ~20% in-market per year and ~5% per quarter. |
| Evidence strength | **Heuristic, and the paper says so**: "The 95% figure is not meant to be a precise rule. We're using it as a heuristic." |
| Applies to small business | **Yes, with a caveat.** The mechanism is interpurchase interval, which any category has. But the 95% figure comes from long-cycle corporate services. A category bought monthly has a completely different ratio. |
| Applies to organic social | Untested here. The mechanism (most of your audience is not buying now) is category logic, not channel logic, so it plausibly transfers — but this paper does not test it. |

**The consequence, which matters more than the number:** advertising cannot mainly
work by prompting a purchase, because most people seeing it will not buy for a year
or more. It must work by building memory links that activate later.

**Directly actionable:** the paper gives a way to compute your own figure rather than
borrowing 95%. Average interpurchase time of two years → ~50% in-market per year,
~13% per quarter. Where that is unknown, one survey question gets it: *"How
frequently do you purchase X?"* with response bands calibrated to the category.

**Second consequence, which is a budgeting rule:** if you spend the whole budget in
one quarter you are off the air for the rest of the year, missing every buyer who
comes into market during it. Spread spend over time.

### C2 — Consideration sets are tiny

**B2B buyers consider about three suppliers on average.**

| | |
|---|---|
| Source | *Why you need to advertise: the best product doesn't win*, Ehrenberg-Bass B2B Report |
| Author | Prof. John Dawes |
| Date | February 2023 |
| What was measured | Two studies cited, one finding an average consideration set of **two**, another **four**; the paper averages them to 3.0. A qualitative Swedish B2B study corroborates small sets. |
| Evidence strength | Measured, but thin — two studies averaged, and the paper does not reconcile why they differ by 2×. |
| Applies to small business | **Yes**, and it is the strongest argument in the file for why a small brand must advertise at all. |
| Applies to organic social | Not tested. |

**The arithmetic that follows is the useful part.** If a buyer considers ~3 brands,
each has ~33% chance of being bought. So making one more person think of you moves
that person from roughly **0% to roughly 33%**.

**Related, same source:** over 80% of people chose a brand they were **already
familiar with** when searching online (Dahlen/RedC cited). The paper's blunt version:
most brands are not rejected for being inadequate — they never make the shortlist at all.

### C3 — Nobody knows what the best product is

**Product superiority does not sell itself, because buyers never compare all the options.**

| | |
|---|---|
| Source | *Why you need to advertise: the best product doesn't win* |
| Author | Prof. John Dawes |
| Date | February 2023 |
| What was measured | Argument built on the consideration-set evidence in C2 plus the economics of "information imperfections". Not a separate measurement. |
| Evidence strength | **Reasoned position supported by measured inputs**, not itself a finding. Label it as such if it is ever cited. |
| Applies to small business | Yes — it directly rebuts the two objections small operators actually make: "our product speaks for itself" and "we're not good enough to promote yet". |
| Applies to organic social | Plausibly. Untested. |

Buyers are "cognitive misers" — they will not spend more thought than necessary. The
paper is explicit that this is **not** an argument for ignoring quality.

### C4 — Unfamiliarity, not dislike, is the real barrier for AI brands

**Generative AI companies are rejected by ~6% of professional users, and the most
common reason is simply not knowing them.**

| | |
|---|---|
| Source | *Buyer says no? The rejection of Generative AI brands in B2B* |
| Authors | Prof. Jenni Romaniuk & Lara Stocchi |
| Date | August 2025 |
| What was measured | 20 companies offering Generative AI products, **2,835 professional users across 13 countries**, 260 observations. Rejection = "I would refuse to buy/use this brand" on a five-part attitude question; reasons coded from open-ended follow-ups into 12 themes. |
| Evidence strength | **Measured, and the best-evidenced row in this file** — real sample, stated method, stated instrument. |
| Applies to small business | **Yes, and more so.** The unaware-to-rejector ratio is 1.3 for the biggest brand and 3.9 for the smallest. Small brands have up to ~290% more people who have never heard of them than people who dislike them. |
| Applies to organic social | Not tested, but the finding is about awareness, which is what organic content is for. |

Key numbers: average rejection **6%**; 90% of rejection levels under 10%; maximum
observed **15%**. Lack of familiarity was the most common rejection reason at **22%**.
Cost, negative word of mouth and negative publicity were each **≤3%**.

**The strategic conclusion, stated by the authors:** there is more upside in building
mental availability than in addressing stated reasons for rejection — and trying to
rebut objections risks amplifying a small issue by raising it to people who never had it.

A useful guard against over-reading: 43% of respondents rejected *at least one* of the
20 companies, so the low per-company rate is not apathy.

### C5 — The frame the whole programme rests on

**Brands grow by being easy to mind and easy to find — mental and physical availability.**

| | |
|---|---|
| Source | *How B2B Brands Grow*, Ehrenberg-Bass with the LinkedIn B2B Institute |
| Authors | Jenni Romaniuk, Byron Sharp, John Dawes, Sahar Faghidno |
| Date | November 2023 |
| What was measured | A compendium bringing existing Ehrenberg-Bass laws to B2B with B2B data. The PDF supplied is the **front matter and contents only** — the substantive chapters were not in it. |
| Evidence strength | **Cannot assess from what we have.** Recorded as a pointer, not as a citable finding. |

Positions taken, per the B2B Institute's introduction: brands grow by **acquiring new
customers**, not by increasing loyalty; brands compete through "meaningless
distinctiveness" (logos, characters, colours) rather than meaningful differentiation.

**Do not cite C5 as evidence.** Get the full report first. It is listed here so nobody
re-derives the reading list.

### C6 — Eye tracking is the wrong instrument for attention

**Heart rate discriminated high from low attention better than eye tracking did.**

| | |
|---|---|
| Source | *What is the best way to measure attention to video advertising?* |
| Authors | Nicole Hartnett, Steven Bellman, Virginia Beal, Rachel Kennedy, Claire Charron, Duane Varan |
| Date | 2025 |
| What was measured | Lab experiment manipulating attention to video ads (high vs low). Eight physiological measures compared against EEG as a non-scalable gold standard, plus self-report. |
| Evidence strength | **Measured, single lab experiment.** Sample size not stated in the abstract we have. |
| Applies to small business | **Not directly** — this is about how attention vendors should measure, not what a marketer should do. |
| Applies to organic social | Same. |

Finding: eye-tracking measures "have problems discriminating high attention from low
attention". Heart rate was best, and can register attention to **sound** as well as visuals.

**Why it is in the file anyway:** it is the citation to reach for whenever someone
quotes an attention statistic sourced from eye tracking. It does not tell you what to
make; it tells you which numbers to distrust.

### C7 — Double Jeopardy: you cannot grow on loyalty

**Loyalty is largely a predictable function of market share. Smaller brands suffer twice — fewer buyers, who are also slightly less loyal.**

| | |
|---|---|
| Source | *The Double Jeopardy Law in B2B shows the way to grow* |
| Authors | Jenni Romaniuk, John Dawes, Sahar Faghidno |
| Date | May 2021 |
| What was measured | Evidence across a range of B2B categories, countries and loyalty metrics. First discovered in the 1960s (McPhee 1963) and replicated in packaged goods, retail banking, insurance, luxury, political voting, car buying, and newer categories like music streaming and ride-share. |
| Evidence strength | **The strongest form of evidence in this file** — a pattern replicated across decades, categories and countries. This is what "law" means in the Ehrenberg-Bass sense: an observed regularity, not a theory. |
| Applies to small business | **Yes, and it is the least comfortable claim here.** It says a small brand's lower loyalty is *normal*, not a failure of relationship-building. |
| Applies to organic social | The law is about purchase behaviour, not content. It sets the goal (more buyers) without saying anything about the channel. |

**The strategic consequence, stated plainly by the authors:** it is not possible to sustainably grow by focusing on loyalty alone. Growth means more customers in any time period.

**What this rules out:** loyalty campaigns as a growth strategy. The paper frames it as what to "stop worrying about / wasting time on."

### C8 — You compete with the biggest brands, whatever your positioning

**Brands share customers with all other brands in proportion to those brands' size — the Duplication of Purchase law.**

| | |
|---|---|
| Source | *How do B2B brands compete?* |
| Author | Prof. Jenni Romaniuk |
| Date | May 2021 |
| What was measured | US business insurance across 16 products (Ehrenberg-Bass data, 2019) — sharing declined in line with penetration at a **93% correlation**. Replicated in B2B aviation fuel contracts (Ehrenberg & Uncles 1990) and UK business banking defection/acquisition over 12 months (2019). |
| Evidence strength | **Measured, multi-category, with a stated correlation.** Originally established in television viewing (Ehrenberg & Goodhardt 1969). |
| Applies to small business | **Yes**, and it has a direct practical instruction (below). |
| Applies to organic social | Not tested. |

**Two things follow that are immediately usable:**

1. **Your future customers are currently customers of bigger brands.** So they already think more about a big competitor and notice its marketing more. The paper's conclusion: strong, distinctive branding in *all* activity is needed to overcome that attention bias.

2. **Stop watching the small look-alike competitor.** The paper is explicit — do not get distracted by smaller competitors who resemble you, unless you have evidence of *excess* customer sharing beyond what their market share predicts. Marketing folklore about differentiation makes this paranoia feel rational; the data says it usually is not.

### C9 — Going dark: the numbers, and the contradiction inside them

**Brands that stopped advertising for a year or more saw sales fall on average 16% after one year and 25% after two.**

| | |
|---|---|
| Source | *What happens when brands stop advertising?* |
| Authors | Adam Gelzinis, Rachel Kennedy, Virginia Beal, Nicole Hartnett, Byron Sharp |
| Date | Released to sponsors 2018; later published in the *Journal of Advertising Research*; opened publicly |
| What was measured | Media spend and volume sales for **70 competitive brands in one Australian consumer goods category over 20+ years**, yielding 57 cases of a brand cutting all mass media for a year or longer (34 continued to a second year). |
| Evidence strength | **Single study, single category, single country — and the paper says so**: "Further research is underway to document the generalisability of these findings beyond this single study." Peer-reviewed, but do not present 16% as a general law. |
| Applies to small business | **Yes, and worse.** Small brands typically suffered greater declines than big ones. Big *growing* brands often kept growing for one to two years after stopping; for small growing brands the trend reversed quickly. |
| Applies to organic social | Not tested. This is paid mass media. |

**The contradiction, which is in the same paper and must travel with the number:** over 150 split-cable tests from the early 1990s to mid-2000s found that **just over half showed no significant sales difference** within a year of stopping TV advertising. The paper's own reading: roughly a 50/50 chance that stopping has no effect inside twelve months, and stability for a year or so is common.

So the honest version is **not** "stop advertising and lose 16%". It is: *there may be a safe period of about a year, after which decline becomes likely, and the smaller and more recently-growing you are, the less safe that period is.*

Anyone quoting the 16% without the 50/50 finding is misrepresenting the source.

### C10 — Brand purpose: almost nobody knows what your purpose is

**Across 14 brands held up as exemplars of purpose-led strategy, average consumer awareness of their stated purpose was 18% — and genuine awareness rarely exceeds 10%.**

| | |
|---|---|
| Source | *Brand Purpose… Are consumers aware to care?* |
| Authors | Victoria Tait, Dr Virginia Beal, Prof. John Dawes, Prof. Byron Sharp |
| Date | ~2025 |
| What was measured | Surveys in the **US, UK and Australia**, covering 14 brands widely regarded as purpose exemplars. |
| Evidence strength | **Measured, and unusually well-controlled** — see the guessing check below. |
| Applies to small business | **Yes, and it saves you money.** If best-in-class purpose brands cannot get past ~10% genuine awareness, a small business will not. |
| Applies to organic social | Not tested, but the finding is about awareness of a message, which is what content is for. |

**Why this row is more trustworthy than most:** the researchers included **dummy purpose attributes** to measure guessing. 4% of UK and 9% of US respondents agreed that Patagonia *"advocates for neuroscience led literacy"* — a thing that does not exist. That implies a guessing rate of roughly 9 percentage points, so the 18% headline is a best case and real awareness rarely clears 10%.

The one exception was Nike (32% UK, 52% US for "promotes diversity and inclusion in sport"), which the authors attribute partly to Nike's very high penetration — **bigger brands score higher on any image perception**.

**The cost argument, which is the actionable half:** investing in purpose awareness comes at the expense of building other memory associations or making the brand easier to buy.

**Worth reading for its own sake:** the lead researcher opens with a disclaimer that she chose the topic *because* she believed in purpose-led brands and shops that way herself — and followed the evidence against her own expectation. That is the standard this knowledge layer should hold itself to.

### C11 — Category Entry Points are the mechanism

**Mental availability is built by linking your brand to the cues buyers actually use when a buying situation arises.**

| | |
|---|---|
| Source | *Category Entry Points in a Business-to-Business (B2B) world* |
| Author | Prof. Jenni Romaniuk |
| Date | July 2022 |
| What was measured | A framework paper — how to identify, prioritise, build and measure CEPs — building on the Double Jeopardy evidence rather than presenting new measurements. |
| Evidence strength | **Method, not a finding.** Cite it for *how to do the work*, not as proof of anything. |
| Applies to small business | Yes — this is the most directly operational document in the set. |
| Applies to organic social | Plausibly the strongest transfer of anything here: a CEP is a buying situation, and content can be built around situations. Untested. |

CEPs are the cues a buyer's memory reaches for in a buying situation — internal (motives, emotions) and external (location, time of day). Every situation is unique, but recurring themes exist, and those are the CEPs. They determine which brands are mentally available at the moment the category is entered, i.e. the initial "go to" list.

**This is where C1 connects to practice.** C1 says advertising works by building memory links that activate later; C11 is the method for deciding *which* links, and how to measure them over time.

**Method document:** [Category Entry Points meeting method](methods/category-entry-points.md)
turns this framework into a marked-source client session.

### C12 — Physical availability in B2B is a marketing problem

**Being easy to find is a growth lever equal to being easy to mind, and in B2B it lives in channels marketing usually does not control.**

| | |
|---|---|
| Source | *Easy to Find: Being Where B2B Buying Happens* |
| Authors | Magda Nenycz-Thiel & Jenni Romaniuk |
| Date | November 2025 |
| What was measured | A framework paper. The supplied PDF is largely summary and framing. |
| Evidence strength | **Method and argument**, resting on the Double Jeopardy body of evidence. |
| Applies to small business | Yes, though the specific channels named (internal sales staff, third-party distributors, trade shows) are enterprise-shaped. |
| Applies to organic social | Indirectly — social is arguably a physical-availability channel as much as a mental one. Untested. |

The authors frame the report as a "rallying cry" for B2B marketers to have input into areas usually owned by sales, product, engineering or IT — because those areas determine whether a brand can actually be found and bought.

## Standing caveat

Every source in this file is **B2B**, and most are long-purchase-cycle B2B. Ari's
clients are small businesses selling to consumers as often as to other businesses, and
the delivery channel is short-form social, not advertising. Nothing here has been shown
to transfer. Where a claim is used in a post, the applicability row must be carried with
it — a B2B finding presented as a general truth is exactly the kind of confident
unsourced claim this layer exists to prevent.
