# Knowledge layer: Ehrenberg-Bass / LinkedIn B2B Institute

**Status:** first entries. Extracted 2026-09-13 from open-access PDFs supplied by Ari.
**Purpose:** the Knowledge layer from `docs/vision.md` — claims with sources attached,
so a generated post can carry `claim → source → date` through `ari-source-gate`
instead of dying at it.

## How to read this file

Every row is a claim we can **cite**, not a claim we have **verified independently**.
The distinction matters: Ehrenberg-Bass is the most empirically serious marketing
institute there is, and it is still one institute with one research programme. Where
a number is a heuristic rather than a measurement, this file says so, because the
source says so.

**Ideas extracted, wording not reproduced.** Short quotes are attributed. Anything
longer is stated in our own words with a page reference so it can be checked.

**Nothing here is about Instagram, short-form video, or consumer content.** Every
source below is B2B. That is a real limit and §Applicability records it per claim
rather than letting it get lost.

## Claims

### C1 — The 95-5 rule

**At any one time, roughly 95% of business buyers are not in the market.**

| | |
|---|---|
| Source | *Advertising effectiveness and the 95-5 rule*, Ehrenberg-Bass B2B Report |
| Author | Prof. John Dawes |
| Date | May 2021 |
| What was measured | Derived from average interpurchase intervals, not surveyed directly. Corporations change principal bank or law firm roughly every five years, giving ~20% in-market per year and ~5% per quarter. |
| Evidence strength | **Heuristic, and the paper says so**: "The 95% figure is not meant to be a precise rule. We're using it as a heuristic." |
| Applies to small business | **Yes, with a caveat.** The mechanism is interpurchase interval, which any category has. But the 95% figure comes from long-cycle corporate services. A category bought monthly has a completely different ratio. |
| Applies to organic social | Untested here. The mechanism (most of your audience is not buying now) is category logic, not channel logic, so it plausibly transfers — but this paper does not test it. |

**The consequence, which matters more than the number:** advertising cannot mainly
work by prompting a purchase, because most people seeing it will not buy for a year
or more. It must work by building memory links that activate later.

**Directly actionable:** the paper gives a way to compute your own figure rather than
borrowing 95%. Average interpurchase time of two years → ~50% in-market per year,
~13% per quarter. Where that is unknown, one survey question gets it: *"How
frequently do you purchase X?"* with response bands calibrated to the category.

**Second consequence, which is a budgeting rule:** if you spend the whole budget in
one quarter you are off the air for the rest of the year, missing every buyer who
comes into market during it. Spread spend over time.

### C2 — Consideration sets are tiny

**B2B buyers consider about three suppliers on average.**

| | |
|---|---|
| Source | *Why you need to advertise: the best product doesn't win*, Ehrenberg-Bass B2B Report |
| Author | Prof. John Dawes |
| Date | February 2023 |
| What was measured | Two studies cited, one finding an average consideration set of **two**, another **four**; the paper averages them to 3.0. A qualitative Swedish B2B study corroborates small sets. |
| Evidence strength | Measured, but thin — two studies averaged, and the paper does not reconcile why they differ by 2×. |
| Applies to small business | **Yes**, and it is the strongest argument in the file for why a small brand must advertise at all. |
| Applies to organic social | Not tested. |

**The arithmetic that follows is the useful part.** If a buyer considers ~3 brands,
each has ~33% chance of being bought. So making one more person think of you moves
that person from roughly **0% to roughly 33%**.

**Related, same source:** over 80% of people chose a brand they were **already
familiar with** when searching online (Dahlen/RedC cited). The paper's blunt version:
most brands are not rejected for being inadequate — they never make the shortlist at all.

### C3 — Nobody knows what the best product is

**Product superiority does not sell itself, because buyers never compare all the options.**

| | |
|---|---|
| Source | *Why you need to advertise: the best product doesn't win* |
| Author | Prof. John Dawes |
| Date | February 2023 |
| What was measured | Argument built on the consideration-set evidence in C2 plus the economics of "information imperfections". Not a separate measurement. |
| Evidence strength | **Reasoned position supported by measured inputs**, not itself a finding. Label it as such if it is ever cited. |
| Applies to small business | Yes — it directly rebuts the two objections small operators actually make: "our product speaks for itself" and "we're not good enough to promote yet". |
| Applies to organic social | Plausibly. Untested. |

Buyers are "cognitive misers" — they will not spend more thought than necessary. The
paper is explicit that this is **not** an argument for ignoring quality.

### C4 — Unfamiliarity, not dislike, is the real barrier for AI brands

**Generative AI companies are rejected by ~6% of professional users, and the most
common reason is simply not knowing them.**

| | |
|---|---|
| Source | *Buyer says no? The rejection of Generative AI brands in B2B* |
| Authors | Prof. Jenni Romaniuk & Lara Stocchi |
| Date | August 2025 |
| What was measured | 20 companies offering Generative AI products, **2,835 professional users across 13 countries**, 260 observations. Rejection = "I would refuse to buy/use this brand" on a five-part attitude question; reasons coded from open-ended follow-ups into 12 themes. |
| Evidence strength | **Measured, and the best-evidenced row in this file** — real sample, stated method, stated instrument. |
| Applies to small business | **Yes, and more so.** The unaware-to-rejector ratio is 1.3 for the biggest brand and 3.9 for the smallest. Small brands have up to ~290% more people who have never heard of them than people who dislike them. |
| Applies to organic social | Not tested, but the finding is about awareness, which is what organic content is for. |

Key numbers: average rejection **6%**; 90% of rejection levels under 10%; maximum
observed **15%**. Lack of familiarity was the most common rejection reason at **22%**.
Cost, negative word of mouth and negative publicity were each **≤3%**.

**The strategic conclusion, stated by the authors:** there is more upside in building
mental availability than in addressing stated reasons for rejection — and trying to
rebut objections risks amplifying a small issue by raising it to people who never had it.

A useful guard against over-reading: 43% of respondents rejected *at least one* of the
20 companies, so the low per-company rate is not apathy.

### C5 — The frame the whole programme rests on

**Brands grow by being easy to mind and easy to find — mental and physical availability.**

| | |
|---|---|
| Source | *How B2B Brands Grow*, Ehrenberg-Bass with the LinkedIn B2B Institute |
| Authors | Jenni Romaniuk, Byron Sharp, John Dawes, Sahar Faghidno |
| Date | November 2023 |
| What was measured | A compendium bringing existing Ehrenberg-Bass laws to B2B with B2B data. The PDF supplied is the **front matter and contents only** — the substantive chapters were not in it. |
| Evidence strength | **Cannot assess from what we have.** Recorded as a pointer, not as a citable finding. |

Positions taken, per the B2B Institute's introduction: brands grow by **acquiring new
customers**, not by increasing loyalty; brands compete through "meaningless
distinctiveness" (logos, characters, colours) rather than meaningful differentiation.

**Do not cite C5 as evidence.** Get the full report first. It is listed here so nobody
re-derives the reading list.

### C6 — Eye tracking is the wrong instrument for attention

**Heart rate discriminated high from low attention better than eye tracking did.**

| | |
|---|---|
| Source | *What is the best way to measure attention to video advertising?* |
| Authors | Nicole Hartnett, Steven Bellman, Virginia Beal, Rachel Kennedy, Claire Charron, Duane Varan |
| Date | 2025 |
| What was measured | Lab experiment manipulating attention to video ads (high vs low). Eight physiological measures compared against EEG as a non-scalable gold standard, plus self-report. |
| Evidence strength | **Measured, single lab experiment.** Sample size not stated in the abstract we have. |
| Applies to small business | **Not directly** — this is about how attention vendors should measure, not what a marketer should do. |
| Applies to organic social | Same. |

Finding: eye-tracking measures "have problems discriminating high attention from low
attention". Heart rate was best, and can register attention to **sound** as well as visuals.

**Why it is in the file anyway:** it is the citation to reach for whenever someone
quotes an attention statistic sourced from eye tracking. It does not tell you what to
make; it tells you which numbers to distrust.

## Standing caveat

Every source in this file is **B2B**, and most are long-purchase-cycle B2B. Ari's
clients are small businesses selling to consumers as often as to other businesses, and
the delivery channel is short-form social, not advertising. Nothing here has been shown
to transfer. Where a claim is used in a post, the applicability row must be carried with
it — a B2B finding presented as a general truth is exactly the kind of confident
unsourced claim this layer exists to prevent.
